Clean Construction & Finishing

The difference between handmade and homemade is usually not the stitching. It is the starting chain pulling tight, the gap at the start of every row, and the ends that work loose after one wash.

The foundation chain fix

Almost every crochet pattern starts with a chain, and almost every crochet piece suffers from it. A foundation chain is worked in a loop — you pull the same yarn around itself repeatedly — while the rest of the fabric is worked into a stitch. Those two structures do not behave the same way. The chain ends up denser, less elastic, and it pulls the bottom edge in tighter than everything above it.

On a scarf you might never notice. On a bag, a wallet or anything with a defined edge, the result is a base that is visibly narrower than the body, and it will not lie flat. It is one of the most common reasons a handmade piece looks homemade, and almost nobody mentions it, because the pattern told you to chain.

The professional fix is to stop making a separate chain. Foundation stitches build the base and the first row at the same time, in one continuous structure, so the bottom edge has exactly the same elasticity as the rest of the fabric. There is no ridge, no tight base, and no second row to hide it.

✓ When it is worth the extra effort On anything with a visible flat edge — bag bases, wallets, cuffs, borders, table pieces — the foundation stitch is not optional if you want a clean finish. On a round piece worked in the round, or a piece that will be lined, a good foundation chain and a steady turning chain are perfectly fine.

Foundation single crochet, step by step

Foundation single crochet — Fsc — is the easiest one to learn, and the one you will use most.

  1. Make a starting loop and place your hook in it as you would for a chain.
  2. Yarn over. This yarn over is what will become the top of the single crochet, and it is the part beginners find confusing. It stays on the hook.
  3. Pull the loop through the starting loop and tighten. You now have two loops on the hook, and the yarn over from step 2 is the third. This is your first single crochet, and the base of the piece is now the same thing as the top of it.
  4. Yarn over, insert the hook into the next loop, yarn over and pull up a loop. You should have three loops on the hook.
  5. Yarn over and pull through all three loops to complete the stitch.
  6. Repeat from step 4. Each stitch forms the next loop, so the foundation and the first row grow together and stay identical in elasticity all the way along.

Two things to watch. First, keep the tension on the yarn over in step 2 consistent with the rest of the row — this is exactly where new foundation chains tighten up. Second, count your stitches as you go, because there is no chain to count against and it is easy to lose one at the ends.

Choosing a foundation stitch

The same principle works for every stitch height. Match the foundation stitch to the body of the work, never to the starting chain you are replacing.

  • Foundation single crochet (Fsc) → single crochet body. The safest and most versatile option, and the one to reach for by default.
  • Foundation half double crochet (Fhdc) → half double or taller body, and for edges that need a little more height.
  • Foundation double crochet (Fdc) → double crochet or taller body, and for bases that need to feel substantial.
  • Foundation treble crochet (Ftr) → lace weight and taller work, where the chain would otherwise be dragging the edge tight.

The rule of thumb: use the foundation version of your main stitch. Any mismatch reintroduces exactly the tension difference you were trying to remove.

💪 A swatch pays for itself Before committing a bag panel or a wallet, make a 5 cm foundation-stitch swatch and let it rest flat overnight. If the bottom edge stays level, the method is right for that yarn. If it cups, your tension is drifting and you have found out in ten minutes rather than after ten hours.

Standing stitches and invisible joins

Almost every crocheter starts each new row with a turning chain — chain one for single crochet, chain two or three for taller stitches — and then works into the first stitch of the row. It is the standard teaching method, and it is a poor one.

That turning chain is a permanent part of your fabric. It sits at a different height to a real stitch, it changes the stitch count at the edge of every single row, and it leaves a visible hole and a lump you can feel. On a knitted-looking rib or a crisp textured panel, the row of turning chains is the first thing the eye finds.

The fix is a standing stitch: work the first stitch of the new row directly, with no chain at all. A standing double crochet is simply a double crochet worked into the top of the stitch from the row below, so the new row begins with a stitch that is identical to every other stitch in it.

  1. Insert the hook into the top of the first stitch of the row below, under both loops.
  2. Yarn over and pull up a loop — two loops on the hook.
  3. Yarn over and pull through two. Three loops. This is the chain that is now part of the stitch.
  4. Yarn over and pull through two, then yarn over and pull through two to complete the standing double crochet.
  5. Work the rest of the row as normal, chaining only at the end to turn.

Because the standing stitch replaces the chain, your stitch count stays the same from row to row. That removes an entire category of counting errors, and it is why the row ends up with a clean, straight edge instead of a scalloped one.

➤ Yes, it feels awkward at first Standing stitches are less forgiving to begin than a turning chain because there is no chain to give you height. Expect to slow down for two or three rows. It is worth it — this is the single change with the biggest visible payoff on an otherwise good piece.

Closing the last round invisibly

A slip stitch to close a round leaves a visible join, sits slightly proud, and can tighten the final round enough to make the edge pucker. When the join is going to be seen, bypass it with a duplicate stitch worked on the back with a darning needle, so the closing stitch is hidden inside the fabric rather than sitting on top of it.

Work the final stitch of the round as normal, but leave it unfinished — do not pull it closed. Thread the tail onto a darning needle, and do not pull it through that last stitch, because that would close the gap you just made. Instead:

  1. Insert the needle under the top loops of the next stitch, from the back, passing it under the same loops that stitch was worked into, so the join mimics the structure of the stitch itself.
  2. Pass the loop through and pull it snug, not tight. The join should sit at the same tension as everything around it.
  3. Continue the duplicate stitch along the row to close the round.
  4. Weave the remaining tail in three directions, as described below.

The test is simple: turn the piece over. If you can see a line where the round closed, the duplicate stitch is too tight or you caught the wrong loops. Done correctly it is indistinguishable from the back.

The three-direction tail weave

There is exactly one way to secure a yarn end so it survives repeated washing, and it is not the way most people do it. The tail has to be woven in three distinct directions, and it has to be woven with a sharp darning needle.

Why the needle matters: a blunt tapestry needle pushes the plies of the yarn apart and leaves an open channel behind it. A tail woven into that channel can be pulled straight back out, which is why blunt-needle ends work loose after a wash. A sharp darning needle splits the fibres and pushes them back around the tail, gripping it in place.

  1. Direction one — along the row. Weave the tail under the tops of several stitches in the same direction as the row, for 3–5 cm. This hides the tail in the structure.
  2. Change direction. Turn the piece, and weave back under the bottoms of the same stitches, at right angles to the first pass. Crossing the first pass locks it.
  3. Change direction again. Weave once more along the row, on the opposite side of the stitch from the first pass. Now the tail is caught in three planes rather than one.
  4. Stretch the fabric gently after each pass. This lets the fibres close around the tail, which is what actually grips it.
  5. Trim close and leave it. Do not knot it — a knot is the first thing to come undone in a wash.
🔒 On the back, and out of sight Every pass should be on the wrong side of the work. If you cannot find a place where three directions all hide inside existing stitches, the tail is long enough to split across a neighbouring row — do that rather than settling for two directions.

Finishing checklist

Before a piece is finished and photographed, run it through this:

  • Base: foundation stitch used, or a starting chain that sits flat after blocking.
  • Row starts: standing stitches, no turning chains left in the fabric.
  • Row ends: turning chains only at the end of the row, and the same number every row.
  • Round closure: duplicate stitch, or a slip stitch buried in a seam that will be covered.
  • Colour joins: trapped inside a stitch, then covered with a standing stitch.
  • Ends: every tail woven in three directions, with a sharp needle, on the wrong side.
  • Blocked: washed or steamed, shaped, and dried completely.
  • Final look: turned over and inspected. Anything you can see from the wrong side should be on the wrong side of the fabric.

None of this is difficult, and all of it is invisible to someone who does not know to look. That is exactly the point. Once the construction is clean, the last thing that separates a good piece from a great one is surface — and that is what texture stitches are for.

Questions

Frequently asked

Why is my starting chain tighter than the rest of the project?

Because the chain is worked in a loop, so you are pulling the same yarn around itself, while the body of the fabric is worked into a stitch. The two sections end up with different elasticity, and the bottom edge pulls in. The fix is to stop making a separate chain: build the foundation and the first row at the same time with a foundation stitch, so the base has exactly the same give as everything above it.

What is a foundation single crochet?

Foundation single crochet, usually abbreviated Fsc, is a single crochet worked directly into the yarn instead of into a pre-made chain. Each stitch forms the next loop, so the foundation and the first row are a single continuous structure. The bottom edge ends up as elastic and even as the rest of the fabric, with no tight base and no ridge.

What is the difference between a standing stitch and a turning chain?

A turning chain means you chain one, two or three at the start of the row and work into the first stitch. That chain becomes a permanent part of the fabric, it sits at a different height to a real stitch, and it leaves a visible gap. A standing stitch means you work the first stitch of the new row directly, with no chain at all, so the row begins with a stitch that matches every other stitch in it.

Why use a sharp darning needle instead of a blunt tapestry needle?

A blunt needle pushes the plies of the yarn apart and leaves a channel for the tail to sit in, so the tail can be pulled back out. A sharp needle splits the fibres and pushes them back around the tail, which grips it in place. That is what makes a woven end survive washing instead of working loose.

How do I hide the join when I change colour or add a new skein?

Work the new yarn into the stitch you are about to work, using the tail as part of the stitch, so both tails are trapped in the fabric at the join. Then start the new row with a standing stitch instead of a chain, so the join is covered by a real stitch. Bypassing the join with a darning needle afterwards, on the back, hides the last trace.
