Texture & Dimension

Colour is the first thing the eye notices, but depth is what makes a piece look expensive. Two substitutions — post stitches and third loop — will change the surface of your crochet without changing the pattern, the yarn or the colours.

Why texture sells the work

Colour is the first thing the eye notices. Texture is the second, and it is the one that separates a handmade piece from a handmade-looking one. Real depth in crochet does not come from a complicated pattern — it comes from moving the stitches relative to each other.

Almost all surface texture in crochet comes from one idea: work the stitch around the body of the stitch below, rather than into the top of it. That single change pulls the stitch forward or pushes it back, and the displacement is what your eye reads as texture. Two techniques do it, and they do it in opposite directions.

🎨 Texture before colour If you are choosing between a new colourway and a new stitch texture, choose the texture. Texture is visible in black and white, it survives being photographed badly, and it does not limit you to one season. It is also the thing that makes a plain colour look deliberate.

Front post and back post

A normal stitch sits on top of the stitch below, entering its two top loops. A post stitch instead wraps around the vertical stem of the stitch below — the part you can see between rows.

  • Front post (FP) — work around the stitch on the side nearest you, pulling it toward you and slightly to the right as you go. The result is a raised, forward-facing column.
  • Back post (BP) — work around the stitch on the side away from you, pushing it back. The result is a recessed groove.

Alternating the two is what produces the classic ribbed, rope-like or cable-like panel. Because the post stitch grips the stem below it rather than resting on the loops, it also stretches the fabric far less than a normal stitch — which is why post stitches are the right choice for a band, a strap or a bag base that has to hold its shape.

Working a post stitch

For a front post double crochet:

  1. Yarn over and insert the hook from front to back, around the vertical stem of the stitch below, between the stitch and the space before it. The hook should come out on the far side of the stem.
  2. Yarn over and pull up a loop — three loops on the hook.
  3. Complete the double crochet as normal: yarn over and pull through two, twice.

For a back post double crochet the only difference is step 1: insert the hook from back to front, around the stem, so the stitch is pushed away from you instead of pulled toward you.

Two practical notes. You will feel a moment of resistance as the hook goes around the stem — that is normal, and easing into it rather than forcing it is what stops the stitch from stretching the fabric. And the stitch behind the post gets slightly narrower, so tuck your hook under both loops of the next normal stitch or you will start to lose loops at the edge.

✓ Post stitches are self-documenting Because a post stitch is a double crochet that has simply moved position, you can use any height — single, half double, double or treble — and the abbreviation just changes (FPs, FPhdc, FPdc, FPtr). The effect scales with the stitch, so a taller post gives a deeper, more architectural groove.

Third loop crochet

A half double crochet is finished with a single yarn over, which means three loops remain on the face of the stitch: the two that form the visible V, and a third one sitting lower, on the back of the stitch.

Crocheters call that third loop the third loop, and it is the one that is usually ignored. When you work the next row into it instead of into the top two loops, you are leaving the V of the stitch below sitting proud and forward instead of pulling it flat into the row. Rows and rows of that produce a braided, knit-like horizontal band across the fabric — the crochet equivalent of a knit rib, from a change of loop and nothing else.

  • Surface: a raised braid-like row with a clear horizontal line
  • Effect: knit-like, ribbed, and visually much richer than flat single crochet
  • Cost: tighter fabric, and a shorter finished piece than the same pattern worked normally

That last point matters. Stitching into the third loop takes less yarn into the stitch, so the fabric comes out denser and the piece measures shorter. Treat it as a texture change that requires a fresh gauge swatch, not a free upgrade.

Working the third loop

  1. Crochet your half double rows as normal. Do not change anything yet.
  2. Turn your work so the third loop is facing you. The V will end up on the back of the finished piece — if you want the braid on the front, work the band the other way round.
  3. Insert the hook under the single loop at the back of the stitch, below the V and above the base of the stitch.
  4. Yarn over and pull up a loop, then complete the stitch as normal.
  5. Work every stitch this way to the end of the row, and continue on subsequent rows.

When you turn the piece back the right way round, the Vs of the half doubles stand up in neat rows and read as a woven braid. It is one of the most satisfying texture changes in crochet, and it takes about a minute per row to learn.

🧵 Find the loop first, always Count up from the base of the hdc: the third loop is the horizontal bar just below the V, not the V itself. If you cannot find it on the first try, work one row and look at the back of the fabric — it is much easier to see from there, and the mistake is easy to undo while it is still loose.

Combining them

Used together, the two techniques give you a full texture vocabulary without adding a single new colour:

  • Bag panel: a base row of back post stitches for structure, a body of third loop single or half double for a knit-like surface, and a top band of front post for a crisp rim.
  • Band or strap: alternating front post and back post double crochet, worked into the base chain rather than into stitches — the posts of the turning chain become the first row of posts, so the band has a clean, ridged edge from day one.
  • Close-fitting garment: a third loop rib at the cuffs and hem, so the rib holds its shape and the body stays soft.
  • Cable-look panel: a repeating block of front post stitches with a back post stitch offset between them, which reads as a raised rope.

On the bags in our own collection we usually combine a post stitch panel with a flat section, so there is a contrast between the two surfaces rather than texture everywhere. Uniform texture reads as a machine finish; varied texture reads as a decision someone made.

Common mistakes

  • Working into only one loop beside the post. The post stitch narrows the stitch behind it, so this silently loses a loop every row. Always take both.
  • Forcing the hook around the stem. Push the hook in and let the stitch come to you. Forcing it stretches the fabric and leaves a permanently wider gap.
  • Mixing post and normal stitches in the same row by accident. Check the count at the end of every row — a single normal stitch among posts is visible as a dent.
  • Forgetting to re-swatch after switching to the third loop. The piece will come out shorter than the pattern, and it will not be a pleasant surprise.
  • Post stitches on very fine yarn and small hooks. There is not enough room to work around the stem. Above roughly 3 mm it becomes easier rather than harder.
  • Using a texture stitch to hide uneven tension. It will not work. Texture magnifies uneven tension rather than disguising it — fix the tension first, in Guide 01.

That is the full set. Four guides, and between them they cover the four things that actually decide how a finished piece feels: how high the stitches sit, how the fabric measures, whether the construction is clean, and whether the surface has depth. Work through them in that order and the rest becomes detail.

Questions

Frequently asked

What are front post and back post stitches?

They are stitches worked around the vertical body of the stitch below instead of into the top loops. Front post works around the stitch toward you, back post works around the stitch behind. Because the stitch wraps the post rather than sitting on top of it, the finished fabric has a raised, ribbed texture instead of a flat surface.

Why use post stitches instead of normal stitches?

Two reasons. First, texture: wrapping the post displaces the stitch in front, which is what creates the crisp raised columns. Second, structure: a post stitch grips the stitch below it far more firmly than a normal stitch, so the fabric is much less likely to stretch. That makes post stitches the right choice for a bag base or a band that has to hold its shape.

What is third loop crochet?

A half double crochet has three loops on its face: the two that form the visible V, and a third one lower down on the back of the stitch. Working into that third loop instead of the top two leaves the V sitting proud at the front, and rows of them read as a braided, knit-like horizontal band. It is the crochet equivalent of a knit rib, achieved by changing only which loop you work into.

Does third loop crochet change my gauge?

Yes. Stitching into the third loop uses less yarn than stitching into the top two, so the fabric comes out tighter and the piece is shorter than the same pattern worked normally. Treat it as a texture change that also requires a fresh gauge swatch, and recalculate the finished size.

Can I use post stitches with a chunky yarn?

Yes, and on chunky yarn they have more effect than on fine yarn because the displacement is larger relative to the stitch. The practical limit is that post stitches need room to work around the post, so on very fine yarn and small hooks they become awkward. Above about 3 mm of yarn or hook they get easier, not harder.
