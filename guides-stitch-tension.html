Controlling the Golden Loop

Two crocheters can follow the exact same pattern, in the exact same yarn, on the exact same hook — and end up with two completely different fabrics. The difference is almost never the pattern. It is the height of the very first loop.

What the golden loop actually is

Every crochet stitch begins the same way. You put the hook into the fabric, catch the yarn, and draw a loop back up through the work. That first loop — the one the pattern never names and never counts — is the one that sets the height of everything above it. Crocheters call it the golden loop, because every stitch after it is built on top of it.

It helps to split tension into two axes, because they are adjusted completely differently:

  • Horizontal tension is how firmly you draw the loop sideways to size it against the hook shaft. This is what most pattern advice and most hook-size charts are actually correcting.
  • Vertical tension is how high you lift that loop before you work it off the hook. This is the half that almost nobody talks about.

You can hold your horizontal tension perfectly steady for fifty rows and still produce stiff, compressed fabric, purely from how high you are riding the loop. That is why experienced crocheters talk about stitch height rather than simply “being loose” or “being tight.”

🧙 The habit worth building Work the first three stitches of every row deliberately, at the height you actually want, then let your hand settle into that rhythm. Muscle memory is faster than willpower, and three stitches is enough to set it.

The three tension types

Most crocheters settle into one of three positions without ever naming it. Knowing which one you are is the whole exercise — because you cannot consciously change what you have never identified.

Yankers — the loop rides low and gets pulled tight

The hook sits low in the fabric and the loop is dragged up snug against the working row before the stitch is completed. The result is dense, stiff fabric with short stitches. The fabric holds a shape beautifully, which is exactly why it is the right choice for structure.

  • Fabric: firm, compact, very good at holding a form
  • Best for: structured bags, wallets, basket bodies, firm bases, anything that needs to hold its shape
  • Watch for: stitch definition disappearing, edges curling, and fabric feeling board-like once it is blocked

Riders — the hook rides level on top of the row

The hook stays level and the loop is drawn up to the same height as the row you are working from. This produces standard, stable, predictable results. It is the default that most patterns assume, which is why most patterns are written for a rider.

  • Fabric: even, balanced, close to gauge
  • Best for: everyday pieces, anything where you want to trust the pattern’s numbers, colourwork, most amigurumi
  • Watch for: the fabric being a little flat and uninteresting — correct, but not distinctive

Lifters — the loop lifts above the working row

The loop is pulled up slightly higher than the row before the stitch is worked off. This gives loose, open, highly flexible fabric with maximum drape. Height costs you a little density, and in exchange you get fabric that moves.

  • Fabric: soft, airy, fluid, excellent drape
  • Best for: luxury lace, shawls and wraps, scarves, light garments, anything where the fabric should fall
  • Watch for: the piece growing longer than the pattern predicts, and stitches looking uneven if you are not deliberate about it
✏ Choose the lift on purpose For every project we decide which of the three we are working as before the first row, and we hold that position for the whole piece. A piece that is a rider for the body and a lifter for the last three rows will show you exactly where the change happened.

Which one your project needs

The honest answer is that the project decides, and it is worth deciding before you start rather than discovering it at the blocking table.

  • Structured bags, wallets, firm bases → anchor as a yanker or a tight rider. The fabric needs to resist, not flow.
  • Everyday pieces where the pattern has a specific finished size → work as a rider and let the pattern’s numbers do the work. Deviating here means recalculating the whole garment.
  • Lace, shawls, wraps, light scarves, drapey garments → work as a lifter. Drape is not something you can add at the end; it is decided stitch by stitch.
  • Anything with a lined interior → rider, or a mild lifter. Very open fabric looks unfinished once it is lined.

And if you are making something to sell, there is a fifth consideration. A slightly tighter, more consistent fabric photographs better, survives shipping better, and feels more valuable in the hand. For the bags and purses in our own collection we work as a firm rider — structured enough to hold its shape, open enough that the stitch pattern stays readable.

Find your natural type in ten minutes

Stop guessing. Do this instead:

  1. Crochet a 15 cm square in your main stitch, in the yarn and hook you actually intend to use. Do not correct anything while you make it. Let your hand do whatever it does.
  2. Block it properly — wash or steam it, then pin or lay it flat and let it dry completely.
  3. Measure it, then hold it. Does it stand up on its own? Is it dense enough that light barely passes through? That is a yanker.
  4. Is it even, with clear stitch definition and no stiffness in either direction? That is a rider.
  5. Is it soft, slightly open, and does it fall into a curve when you let it hang? That is a lifter.
  6. Then make the same square again, deliberately working the other way. Compare the two side by side. That comparison is the single most useful thing in this entire guide, because it turns an instinct into a choice.
📌 Measure more than you count Write down three numbers for every swatch: stitches per 10 cm, rows per 10 cm, and the blocked measurement. Most of the time a gauge “mismatch” is a row-count problem caused by stitch height, not a stitch-count problem.

Switching on purpose

Once you know your default, moving between the three is a small physical adjustment rather than a mindset change:

  • Rider → Lifter: insert the hook less deeply, so you are not pulling the new loop up through the full height of the stitch. This alone lifts the loop and opens the fabric.
  • Lifter → Rider: insert the hook fully, catching both loops of the stitch rather than entering under the first loop only. Taking more yarn out of the fabric tightens it.
  • Any → Yanker: draw the loop up snug against the working row before you complete the stitch, and keep the hook riding low as you work.
  • Any → anything: change your grip. A loose pinch on the yarn lets you lift higher; pinching the yarn close to the hook gives you finer, tighter control. This is the least obvious lever and often the most effective one.

Reading your finished fabric

Once you can name the three types, fabric problems stop being mysterious. These are the four you will actually meet:

  • Stiff, board-like, edges curling inward. Too much yanker. Lift the loop slightly and let the fabric open up.
  • See-through, slouchy, growing longer than the pattern. Too much lifter. Ride level and take more yarn out of each stitch.
  • Rows that get narrower as you go, or a cone shape instead of a rectangle. Tension is drifting mid-row. This is a grip and posture problem more than a technique one — rest the hand, reset with one deliberate stitch, and carry it through.
  • The first few rows of every piece look great, then it falls apart. You are crocheting your “best” stitches before you have relaxed into the project. Expect your hands to settle into a different, more relaxed tension by row ten, and set your gauge from the middle of the swatch, not the top.

Once your tension is where you want it, the next thing to get right is the gauge itself — and the hook you are holding is a bigger variable than most pattern books admit.

Questions

Frequently asked

What is the golden loop in crochet?

It is the very first loop you pull up through the fabric, before you yarn over and work the stitch off the hook. Because every stitch above it is built on that loop, its height sets the height of the whole fabric. Most patterns never mention it, which is why the same written pattern can produce stiff fabric in one pair of hands and draping fabric in another.

Am I a yanker, a rider or a lifter?

Make a 15 cm square with whatever your hand does naturally, then block it. If the fabric is dense, firm and barely drapes, you are a yanker. If it is even, stable and holds a shape without being rigid, you are a rider. If it is soft, open and drapes noticeably, you are a lifter. Almost everyone lands hardest in one of the three.

Why does my crochet look stiff even though I am being careful?

Stiffness is nearly always vertical tension, not effort. If the hook rides low and the loop is drawn up tight before you complete the stitch, the fabric compresses. Deliberately riding level or lifting slightly above the working row will open the same fabric up without changing hook size, yarn or pattern.

Does stitch height change the finished size?

Yes, and this is the part that catches people out. Tighter, shorter stitches mean more stitches fit into the same measurement, so a swatch that looks comfortable can still come out smaller than the pattern assumes. Always check the stitch count per 10 cm, not only the row count, before trusting a measurement.

How do I even out tension mid-project?

Do not fight it in the middle of a row. Finish the row, then check the last few stitches against the first few. If they differ, the tension drifted rather than jumped. Rest your hook hand, take one stitch deliberately at the height you want, and use that stitch as the reference for the rest of the row.
